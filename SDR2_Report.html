Does Visual Perception in a Social Robot Nutrition Assistant Improve User Experience?

NAO Robot HRI System — LLM-Powered, Memory-Augmented Nutrition Interaction

📅 Deadline: 14 May 2026 📝 Max 1500 words 👥 Group Submission 🎓 University of Bristol
1

Introduction

Social robots are increasingly being explored as tools for health promotion, offering a uniquely embodied and conversational mode of interaction that screen-based applications cannot replicate (Fong et al., 2003). The NAO humanoid robot, with its expressive limb movements, synthesised speech, and on-board camera, presents a promising platform for delivering personalised health guidance. Nutrition is a domain well suited to conversational robot assistance: dietary habits are deeply personal, users benefit from contextualised advice, and the conversational format supports sustained engagement (Bickmore & Picard, 2005).

A key capability distinguishing advanced social robots from simple chatbot interfaces is multimodal perception — in particular, the ability to visually observe a user's environment in real time. When a user holds up a meal and asks "Is this healthy?", a vision-enabled robot can ground its response in what it actually sees, rather than relying solely on verbal description. This visual grounding may fundamentally alter how users perceive the robot: as more attentive, more competent, and more socially present. Social presence — the sense of interacting with a real, aware entity — is a strong predictor of user satisfaction and engagement in HRI (Heerink et al., 2010).

Despite its relevance, the isolated effect of visual perception capability on user experience in health-oriented HRI has not been directly studied. This study addresses that gap by implementing two conditions on a NAO robot nutrition assistant — one with live visual analysis, one without — and comparing user ratings on perceived intelligence, helpfulness, and social presence.

2

Research Questions and Hypotheses

Research Questions

RQ1: Does visual perception capability in a social robot nutrition assistant improve users' ratings of the robot's perceived intelligence and helpfulness compared to a text-only baseline?

RQ2: Does visual perception capability increase users' sense of social presence during an interaction with a social robot nutrition assistant?

Hypotheses

H1 — Perceived Intelligence

Users interacting with the vision-enabled robot (Condition B) will rate the robot's perceived intelligence significantly higher than users in the text-only condition (Condition A), as measured by the Godspeed Perceived Intelligence subscale (Bartneck et al., 2009).

Justification: Robots that demonstrate environmental awareness are consistently rated as more intelligent in HRI studies (Mutlu et al., 2009). The ability to identify a food item from a camera image — without being told what it is — is a salient demonstration of perceptual competence.

H2 — Perceived Helpfulness

Users in Condition B will report significantly higher perceived helpfulness scores, as the robot can directly analyse food items rather than relying on user descriptions, enabling more targeted nutritional advice.

H3 — Social Presence

Users in Condition B will report higher social presence scores, as the robot's act of visually attending to the user's environment — an analogue of human gaze — is a key social signal associated with attentiveness and engagement (Kendon, 1967).

System Design

3.1 Dialogue Pipeline

The system operates across three networked layers, enabling real-time spoken conversation between a user and a NAO humanoid robot, with every response generated dynamically by a cloud-hosted large language model (Qwen, Alibaba DashScope). Unlike scripted or rule-based HRI systems, no responses are pre-written — the robot produces contextually appropriate replies conditioned on the user's full session history, dietary log, and (in Condition B) a live camera image.

3.2 Non-Verbal Communication Behaviours

Rather than a fixed keyword-to-gesture rule map, the LLM itself selects the most contextually fitting gesture tag per response. The server extracts the tag via regular expression and dispatches the corresponding NAO animation in a dedicated thread running in parallel with speech synthesis, so gesture and speech are fully synchronised.

The robot draws from 22 gesture behaviours including: greeting, wave, nod, think, explain, point, thumbsUp, celebrate, sorry, sad, confused, bow, stretch, dance, clap, and neutral. This AI-driven selection produces more natural and contextually coherent embodied communication than rule-based approaches.

3.3 Human Perception and Autonomous Visual Response

The experimental manipulation centres on the robot's capacity to perceive its physical environment. The system continuously monitors user speech for vision-trigger keywords. The two conditions produce markedly different robot behaviours in response to the same spoken prompt:

Condition A — Control (no_vision)
  • Vision keyword detected but ignored
  • NAO says: "I cannot see things around me. Please tell me what you have."
  • No camera image captured
  • Server enforces image_data = null
  • Routes to qwen-plus text model
  • No visual persona injected in prompt
Condition B — Experimental (vision)
  • Vision keyword triggers autonomous capture
  • NAO says: "Let me take a look."
  • VGA 640×480 JPEG captured via ALVideoDevice
  • Base64-encoded image (~20–50 KB) sent to server
  • Routes to qwen-vl-plus vision-language model
  • VISION_PERSONA prompt appended: instructs LLM to naturally comment on visible food
Double-layer condition control: The EXPERIMENT_CONDITION flag is set in both robot_client.py (hardware layer) and server_brain.py (AI layer) independently — preventing any cross-contamination between conditions even if the hardware behaves unexpectedly.

Study Methodology

4.1 User Study Design

A between-subjects experimental design was employed with two conditions. The independent variable is visual perception capability (absent vs. present). Sessions were conducted in a quiet room with a table, chair, and three standardised food props (an apple, a packaged sandwich, a chocolate bar) available for Task 3. Participants were randomly assigned to one condition.

Condition Label Robot Capability
A no_vision — Control Text dialogue only; camera disabled
B vision — Experimental Text dialogue + live visual food analysis via Qwen-VL

4.2 User Study Procedure

Each session lasted 15–20 minutes and followed a standardised protocol:

1. Briefing (2 min) — The researcher explained the study and obtained written informed consent.

2. Six standardised interaction tasks (10 min) — Participants received a printed task card and spoke each prompt to NAO in sequence:

Task Participant Prompt Purpose
T1 "Hi Nao, nice to meet you. What can you help me with today?" Greeting; baseline social behaviour
T2 "I had two eggs and a piece of toast for breakfast this morning." Food logging; triggers memory system
T3 ★ [Hold up food prop] "Nao, look — is this a healthy choice for my lunch?" Critical manipulation task — vision vs. no-vision response
T4 "Based on what I've eaten today, what would you recommend for dinner?" Personalised memory-based advice
T5 "I'm trying to eat healthier. What are the most important nutrients I should focus on?" Knowledge depth; sustained dialogue
T6 "Thank you Nao, that was really helpful. Goodbye!" Farewell behaviour

Task T3 is the key between-condition manipulation: Condition B participants receive a visually grounded response naming the specific food item held up; Condition A participants receive verbal-only advice without visual identification.

3. Questionnaire (3 min) — Godspeed questionnaire and Perceived Helpfulness Scale completed immediately after the interaction.

4. Post-study interview (3–5 min) — Semi-structured interview exploring subjective impressions.

5. Debriefing — The researcher explained both conditions and the camera system.

4.3 Dependent Measures

Godspeed Questionnaire (Bartneck et al., 2009): A validated 5-factor HRI instrument measuring Anthropomorphism, Animacy, Likeability, Perceived Intelligence, and Perceived Safety on 5-point semantic differential scales. The Perceived Intelligence subscale (5 items: ignorant–knowledgeable, irresponsible–responsible, unintelligent–intelligent, foolish–sensible, incompetent–competent) is the primary measure for H1. The Animacy and Likeability subscales (8 items combined) serve as a proxy for social presence (H3).

Perceived Helpfulness Scale (custom, 5 items, 7-point Likert): "The robot's advice was relevant to my situation"; "The robot's responses felt personalised to me"; "I would trust this robot's nutritional advice"; "The robot understood what I was asking"; "I found this interaction useful for my nutrition goals." Internal consistency (Cronbach's α) will be reported. Primary outcome for H2.

Post-study interview: Three open questions: (1) "How did you feel about the robot's responses overall?"; (2) "Was there a moment that felt particularly natural or unnatural?"; (3) "Would you use a robot like this for nutrition advice in daily life? Why or why not?" Responses were audio-recorded and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

4.4 Participants

A target of N = 16 participants (8 per condition) were recruited via convenience sampling from University of Bristol students and staff. Inclusion criteria: aged 18–40, fluent English speakers, no prior direct experience with NAO robots. Participants received no financial compensation. Ethical approval was obtained prior to data collection.
